Same machine, two ASINs — and one price is wrong.

Search for a Kismile nugget ice maker on Amazon and you can land on two listings with the identical product name, the identical stainless-steel photos and the identical “35 lbs/Day” headline claim. They are not the same URL. They are two separate ASINs, and as of our last price check they were not even selling for the same amount. That is not a glitch in Amazon's search results. It is a normal, mostly harmless, occasionally confusing part of how this category is sold — and once you know what causes it, it takes about thirty seconds to check.

Here is the situation, stated plainly, because the plain version is already strange enough. There are two Amazon listings for a product called “Kismile Nugget Ice Maker Countertop, 35 lbs/Day, Stainless Steel.” One carries the ASIN B0GS552T5S. The other carries the ASIN B0F64WHP74. The product name is word-for-word identical on both. The stated output — 35 lbs of nugget ice a day — is identical on both. The finish, stainless steel, is identical on both. And on the dates we checked, the price was not identical at all: $144.98 for one, $189.99 for the other, a $45.01 gap for what reads, on paper, as the exact same appliance.

If you've shopped for small kitchen appliances on Amazon for more than a few minutes, you've probably run into some version of this before, even if you didn't stop to name it. A listing you bookmarked yesterday is gone today, replaced by what looks like the same product at a new ASIN. A search turns up two nearly identical tiles and you genuinely cannot tell which one to click. This guide is about that specific, common, and almost always boring phenomenon: why it happens, what it does and doesn't tell you about the seller, how to check two listings yourself before you buy, and — using these two Kismile ASINs and one genuinely different machine as the worked example — which listing actually makes sense to click.

What's actually on the page

Two ASINs, one product page's worth of text

Strip away the ASIN in the URL and the two Kismile listings read as the same page copied twice. Same title: “Kismile Nugget Ice Maker Countertop, 35 lbs/Day, Stainless Steel.” Same headline capacity claim: 35 lbs/Day. Same material callout: stainless steel. Neither listing's title, bullet summary or size/variant line states a single differentiating feature — no mention of a different tank size, a different control panel, a different accessory bundle, nothing. If there is a hardware difference between what ships under B0GS552T5S and what ships under B0F64WHP74, it is not written down anywhere we have access to, which means we're not going to invent one. The honest statement is that these two ASINs describe themselves, in every field we can see, as the same machine.

The mechanics

Why a seller relists an identical product under a second ASIN

Amazon's catalogue is built around the idea that one ASIN equals one distinct product. In practice, sellers end up creating a second ASIN for what is functionally the same item for reasons that have almost nothing to do with the product itself and almost everything to do with how the listing is managed on the back end.

The most common reason is a lost listing. If a product page accumulates enough policy violations, intellectual-property complaints, or a suspended Buy Box, the simplest fix for a seller is often not to resolve the underlying issue but to create a fresh ASIN and relaunch the same physical product there, sometimes under a slightly different seller account. A second common reason is multi-seller friction: a brand sells through more than one Amazon seller account, or through a mix of first-party and third-party arrangements, and each account's own listing gets its own ASIN rather than sharing one. A third is simply testing — sellers routinely spin up a second listing to try a different price point, a different set of product photos, or a different advertising campaign, without merging it back into the original once the test is over.

None of this requires anything to be wrong with the product. It is closer to how a budget-tier brand runs its Amazon presence than to anything about the machine inside the box. If you've compared sub-$100 nugget ice makers before, you've likely already noticed that the brands at this price point tend to have messier, more duplicated, more frequently-reshuffled catalogues than an established name like GE or Frigidaire — smaller operations simply have fewer people managing the listing side of the business, and duplicate ASINs are one of the more visible symptoms of that.

The question people actually ask

What this does — and does not — say about counterfeit risk

A duplicate listing is not, by itself, evidence of a counterfeit. The overwhelming majority of duplicate ASINs on Amazon exist because of the catalogue-management reasons above — a relisted product, a second seller account, a pricing experiment — and not because a different factory is shipping a fake unit under the same name. Treating “this listing has a twin” as proof of anything sinister would be its own kind of invented claim, and we're not going to make one we can't back up.

What a duplicate listing does mean is that you should check who is actually selling and shipping each specific ASIN before you buy, because that information can differ between two otherwise identical-looking listings even when the manufacturer name stays the same. Look at the “Sold by” and “Ships from” line on the buy box of each ASIN. If one listing is sold and shipped by Amazon.com directly, or by a storefront that matches the brand name exactly, and the other is sold by a third-party name you've never heard of with no storefront history, that's a real, checkable difference worth weighing — not because duplication itself is the red flag, but because an unfamiliar third-party seller carries its own ordinary risks (slower returns, less responsive support) regardless of whether the listing is a duplicate. The duplication is the symptom that sends you looking; the seller information is the actual signal.

Do this before you buy

How to check whether two listings are really the same machine

You don't need a tool for this, and you don't need to guess. Amazon listings carry enough fields that a side-by-side read tells you almost everything in a couple of minutes. Here's the order we'd check them in, using the two Kismile ASINs above as the worked example:

  1. Read the title character by character. Both Kismile listings use the identical string, “Kismile Nugget Ice Maker Countertop, 35 lbs/Day, Stainless Steel.” If a second listing's title adds a word — a capacity number, a color, a bundle item, a generation marker like Frigidaire's “2.0 Gen” — that's a real difference worth reading further into, not a duplicate.
  2. Compare the stated capacity and size/variant line, not just the bullet points. Both Kismile listings publish the same 35lbs/day size/variant. If we don't have a size or variant line for a product at all, that's a sign to go check the live listing directly rather than assume a spec we can't confirm — see how to read a daily-output claim for the broader version of this caution.
  3. Check “Sold by” and “Ships from” on each ASIN separately. This is the field most likely to actually differ between duplicates, and it's the one that matters most for your own buying decision.
  4. Compare the price on the day you're actually buying. Don't assume duplicate listings are priced the same just because the product description is. Ours weren't — $144.98 against $189.99, a gap wide enough that it would be a mistake to click the first result without checking the other.
  5. Look at the product images for anything the title doesn't mention. A different cord length, a different control panel layout, or an extra accessory in the box photo can indicate the listings have genuinely diverged over time, even if the title text was never updated to match.

Run through that list and one of two things will be true. Either every field lines up, in which case you're almost certainly looking at the same physical product sold through two separate catalogue entries — our situation here — or something doesn't line up, in which case you've just found a real difference the headline title was hiding, and you should read the rest of that specific listing before buying either one.

The actual decision

Which listing to click, and why it rarely matters which

Once you've confirmed, using the checklist above, that two listings describe the same physical product — which is what we found here — the machine you end up with rarely depends on which ASIN you clicked. Same name, same stated capacity, same material, no feature either listing claims that the other doesn't. In terms of what shows up on your counter, it genuinely does not matter whether the box says B0GS552T5S or B0F64WHP74 on the packing label.

What does matter, and what our own price check on these two ASINs happens to demonstrate well, is that duplicate listings are not kept in sync on price. Amazon doesn't merge or average pricing across ASINs that describe the same product; each one is priced independently by whoever controls it, which is exactly how we ended up looking at $144.98 on one Kismile listing and $189.99 on the other for what reads as the identical machine. The practical rule that follows from that is simple: once you've verified two listings are duplicates, open both, compare the current price on each, and buy whichever one is cheaper that day. There is no loyalty-card reason to pay $45 more for the listing you happened to click first.

The one exception worth flagging: if your checklist turns up a real difference in the “Sold by” field — one ASIN fulfilled by Amazon directly, the other by an unfamiliar third party with no storefront history — that's a legitimate reason to pay a small premium for the more reliably-fulfilled listing, even on a true duplicate. Price is the deciding factor only once you've ruled out a seller-reliability difference first. FAQ

Common questions

Are the two Kismile listings actually the same machine?

As far as the published data lets us tell, yes. Both ASINs — B0GS552T5S and B0F64WHP74 — use the identical product name, “Kismile Nugget Ice Maker Countertop, 35 lbs/Day, Stainless Steel,” the identical stated capacity, and the identical stainless-steel finish. Neither listing's title or size/variant field names a single feature the other doesn't also claim. That's the pattern of a duplicate listing, not two genuinely different products.

Is a duplicate listing a sign the product is counterfeit?

No, not by itself. Most duplicate ASINs exist because of ordinary Amazon catalogue mechanics — a relisted product after a suspended listing, a second seller account for the same brand, a price or photo test — and have nothing to do with where the physical unit was made. What's worth checking on a duplicate is the “Sold by” and “Ships from” field on each ASIN, since that can genuinely differ between two listings even when the product description doesn't.

How can I tell if two Amazon listings are really the same product?

Read the title character by character, compare the stated capacity or size/variant line, check whether either listing's bullets or photos name a feature the other is silent on, and compare the “Sold by” field. If every one of those lines up, as they do for the two Kismile ASINs here, you're looking at a duplicate. If any of them diverges — a different word in the title, a different stated capacity — you've found a real difference worth reading into before you buy either one.

Does it matter which Kismile listing I buy?

Not for the machine you'll receive — by every field we can check, it's the same product either way. It does matter for price: our two check dates showed $144.98 on one ASIN and $189.99 on the other, a $45.01 gap that Amazon does nothing to close automatically. Open both listings, compare the current price, and buy the cheaper one, unless the “Sold by” field on the cheaper listing gives you a specific reason not to.
