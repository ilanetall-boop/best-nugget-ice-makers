If you searched "EUHOMY ice maker" and started scrolling, you've probably already seen the problem: several countertop units from the same brand, in nearly the same black plastic housing, at nearly the same size, for prices that range from under seventy dollars to two hundred. The listing photos don't do much to separate them — same angle, same kitchen-counter staging, same carry handle. What's actually different, and different enough to matter the moment you make your first batch, is the ice itself. Two of these EUHOMY machines are bullet ice makers: small, hard, hollow cylinders, the kind that have been in countertop and under-counter ice machines for decades. One is a nugget ice maker: soft, chewable, compacted flakes, the newer category this site is built around.

This isn't a defect in EUHOMY's listings so much as a predictable result of how Amazon search works: a shopper typing "nugget ice maker" sees bullet-ice machines mixed into the results because the two product types share a brand, a price range and a general shape, and the word "nugget" doesn't always appear where you'd expect it to. Below, we go through what each of the three listings actually states — the exact phrase used for output, the exact phrase used for ice type, and what each gallery shows — so the difference is explicit instead of something you discover with a glass in your hand.

The core difference

Two different mechanisms, sold under one brand name

Bullet ice and nugget ice are not two sizes of the same thing — they come from two different pieces of machinery. A bullet ice maker freezes water against a set of small vertical cylinders, then pushes each finished piece out, producing the small hollow-cored "bullet" shape you'll recognize from hotel ice machines and most compact countertop units. It is hard, it is solid, and it holds its shape in a drink for a long time, which is exactly what you want in a glass of iced tea you're not drinking in five minutes.

A nugget ice maker works on a different principle: an auger inside a freezing cylinder continuously scrapes a thin layer of ice off the wall and compacts it under pressure into a soft, porous, irregularly-shaped pellet. That compaction is what makes the ice chewable and lets it absorb the flavor of whatever it's sitting in — the entire reason nugget ice has its own fan base and its own buying guides. Of the three EUHOMY listings we compared, exactly one names this mechanism directly: the EUHOMY Nugget Ice Maker Countertop (ASIN B0GXB6VJCY), which states 44 lbs/24H and ice ready in 7–10 minutes. The other two don't use the word "nugget" anywhere in the title, because they're not nugget machines at all.

The giveaway phrase

“9 cubes ready in 6 minutes” is a bullet-ice claim, not a nugget-ice one

If you remember one phrase from this guide, make it this one. Two EUHOMY listings — the EUHOMY Ice Maker Countertop (ASIN B0DCJ78D27, $63.28) and the EUHOMY Countertop Ice Maker Machine with Handle (ASIN B0BWHZJHPL, $69.99) — both headline with 9 cubes ready in 6 minutes and a stated output of 26 lbs/24H. That phrasing, "cubes" counted individually and ready in a single short cycle, describes a batch of discrete bullet-shaped pieces coming off a small set of freezing fingers. It is not how nugget machines describe themselves, because nugget machines don't make a countable batch of individual cubes — they extrude a continuous stream of compacted ice that fills the bin gradually, which is why the nugget listing above states a rate (44 lbs/24H) and a time to first ice (7–10 minutes) instead of a cube count.

The B0DCJ78D27 listing makes this unambiguous on its own: its title closes with the phrase "2 Sizes of Bullet Ice," naming the ice type outright. The B0BWHZJHPL listing never uses the word "bullet" anywhere in its title — it's titled around the carry handle and the Camping/RV use case instead — but it states the identical spec pair, 26 lbs in 24 hours and 9 cubes ready in 6 minutes, plus the same self-cleaning function and the same basket-and-scoop accessories. Two listings with matching numbers and matching accessories are, functionally, the same category of machine under two different pieces of cover copy. The lesson generalizes: whenever a countertop ice maker's headline spec is a cube count tied to a single-digit minute mark, read it as a bullet-ice claim, regardless of whether the word "bullet" made it into the title.

What the title won't say

Reading the ice shape out of the photos, because the title often won't tell you

As the B0BWHZJHPL listing shows, you can't always rely on the title to name the ice type — the word "bullet" is optional marketing copy, not a required field, and plenty of sellers leave it out. What's harder to fudge is the close-up image further into the gallery that shows ice sitting in the scoop or the bin. The exterior housing shot at the top of the listing — the black countertop box with a lid and a carry handle — looks almost identical across all three EUHOMY machines in this comparison, which is exactly the problem this guide exists to solve. The ice close-up is where the two categories stop looking alike.

Bullet ice photographs as small, roughly cylindrical, semi-translucent pieces, usually with a visible hollow center where the cylinder didn't freeze all the way through — miniature versions of the ice a soda fountain dispenses. Nugget ice photographs as soft, opaque white, irregularly clumped pellets with no visible internal hole, closer in look to compacted snow than to a cube. A visible hole or clean cylindrical edge means bullet ice no matter what the title says; rounded, uneven white granules mean nugget. When the photography is ambiguous or low-resolution, treat the title's wording as the tie-breaker, since that's a text claim the seller is accountable for in a way a stock photo isn't.

The number that doesn't help

Capacity numbers that look similar but describe different machines

It's tempting to use the daily-output figure as a shortcut — bigger number, bigger or better machine — but that number tells you nothing about which ice type you're getting, and the three listings here prove it. The two bullet machines state 26 lbs/24H; the nugget machine states 44 lbs/24H. Those are genuinely different figures, but neither is higher because it's "more nugget" or "more bullet" — they're different because they're different mechanisms at different physical scales, the same way a toaster and a toaster oven both heat bread but the wattage rating doesn't tell you which one you bought.

A shopper comparing EUHOMY listings purely on the lbs/24H line might reasonably conclude the 44 lbs machine is simply "the bigger version" of the 26 lbs ones, upgrade their cart to it assuming more capacity in the same bullet format, and be surprised when soft chewable pellets come out instead of hard cylindrical cubes — or the reverse, ending up with bullet ice when chewable pellets were the whole point of the purchase. The output number is worth comparing once you already know which ice type you want; it is not a reliable way to figure out which ice type you're looking at in the first place. For that, you need the mechanism language above — the word "nugget" in the title, the cube-count-plus-minutes phrasing, or the ice shape in the gallery photo.

How the order goes wrong

Why a buyer who wanted chewable ice ends up with hard bullet cubes

The failure mode here is specific and avoidable once you see it laid out. Someone searches "nugget ice maker" because they want the soft, chewable, Sonic-style ice for iced coffee or cocktails. Amazon's search and recommendation panels mix in adjacent listings from brands that also sell bullet machines, because the algorithm is matching on brand, price band and category tags, not on ice mechanism. The shopper lands on a $63–$70 EUHOMY listing that mentions "Portable Ice Maker Machine," "Self-Cleaning" or "Auto-Cleaning," and a carry handle — all phrases that also appear, almost verbatim, on the actual nugget listing further down the results. Nothing about the surrounding copy signals a problem, the price looks right for an entry-level countertop unit, and the photo of the machine itself looks like every other countertop ice maker shot on a kitchen counter.

The only signals that would have caught it are the ones this guide has walked through: the title says "9 cubes ready in 6 minutes" instead of naming "nugget" anywhere, and the stated output is 26 lbs/24H rather than the 44 lbs/24H the real nugget listing states. Buy on price and exterior photo alone, and the ice that comes out the first time you run it is small, hard and hollow-cored — perfectly good ice for a cooler or a pitcher of tea, and not at all what someone chasing chewable nugget ice set out to buy. The fix is checking two things before adding to cart: does the title contain the word "nugget," and does the output figure pair with a time-to-first-ice framing rather than a cube count. Both take under a minute on the actual product page.

FAQ

Common questions

How can I tell if an EUHOMY listing is nugget or bullet ice?

Check the title first for the word "nugget" — the EUHOMY Nugget Ice Maker Countertop (B0GXB6VJCY) states it outright. If that word is absent, check the spec framing: a cube count paired with a single-digit minute mark ("9 cubes ready in 6 minutes") describes a bullet machine, while a daily-rate figure paired with a time-to-first-ice range (like "44 lbs/24H, ready in 7–10 minutes") describes a nugget machine. If both are ambiguous, look at the ice in the gallery photos: a visible hole or cylindrical edge means bullet ice, soft irregular white granules mean nugget ice.

Why doesn't every bullet-ice listing from EUHOMY say "bullet" in the title?

Because sellers aren't required to name the ice mechanism, and some titles lead with other selling points instead. The EUHOMY Countertop Ice Maker Machine with Handle (B0BWHZJHPL) never uses the word "bullet," leading instead with the carry handle and Camping/RV use case, even though it states the identical 26 lbs/24H and 9-cubes-in-6-minutes specs as the EUHOMY listing that does name its ice type (B0DCJ78D27). Matching specs across listings is a reliable way to identify the same underlying mechanism even when the cover copy differs.

Is "9 cubes ready in 6 minutes" ever used for nugget ice?

Not in the listings we compared here. Nugget machines extrude a continuous stream of compacted ice rather than finishing a small discrete batch, so manufacturers describe them with a daily output rate and a time-to-first-ice figure instead of a cube count. The EUHOMY Nugget Ice Maker Countertop states 44 lbs/24H and ready in 7–10 minutes — no cube count anywhere in its specs.

Does a higher lbs/24H number mean a better or more "upgraded" machine?

Not reliably, and not across ice types. The nugget listing here states a higher output (44 lbs/24H) than either bullet listing (26 lbs/24H each), but that's a function of the two being different mechanisms at different scales, not the nugget machine being an "upgraded" version of the bullet ones. Compare output numbers only after you've confirmed you're looking at machines of the same ice type.
