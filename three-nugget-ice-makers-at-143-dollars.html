Comparison · Three Countertop Nugget Ice Makers Near $130

Ten dollars separates all three. The machines inside do not match that closely.

A Silonn, a Typhur and a EUHOMY countertop nugget ice maker currently list within $10.23 of each other, and the two in the middle of that range are just $2.55 apart. Shop by sticker price and you'd assume they're interchangeable. Their stated daily output runs from 33 lbs to 40 lbs, their water tanks are quoted in two different units, and one of the three names is brand new to the category. We compared what each listing actually states instead of trusting the price tag to tell the whole story.

Three nugget ice makers landed in almost the same price band this week, and two of the three names are ones anyone who's shopped this category before will recognize. The Silonn Nugget Ice Maker Countertop lists at $127.47. The EUHOMY Nugget Ice Maker Countertop with Handle lists at $137.70. Between them, at $135.15, sits a name most shoppers haven't seen attached to a countertop appliance before: Typhur. Across the full set, the spread is $10.23 — tight enough that sticker price alone won't tell you much. The two machines closest together, Typhur and EUHOMY, are separated by just $2.55.

That narrow a band is exactly the situation where a stated daily-output number stops being useful on its own. Silonn claims 33 lbs of ice a day. Typhur claims 35 lbs in 24 hours. EUHOMY claims a maximum of 40 lbs daily. None of those figures differ enough, on a kitchen appliance nobody runs unattended for a full day anyway, to single-handedly justify a purchase at $127 to $138. What actually separates these three is what each brand spent the rest of that money on: a cleaning system, a water tank, a name you can look up elsewhere, or a faster first batch. We compared all four, the same way we did for a different trio of same-priced machines in two listings of the same GE Profile Opal 2.0 Ultra sitting $20.99 apart — another case where a price gap that looks meaningful turns out to hinge on one or two specific, checkable details rather than the headline number.

The first number

Thirty-three, thirty-five, forty pounds a day — and what each one actually costs per pound

Line the three output claims up against their prices and a pattern falls out that the raw dollar figures hide. Silonn's $127.47 buys a stated 33 lbs a day, which works out to about $3.86 for every pound of that daily claim. Typhur's $135.15 buys 35 lbs a day — also about $3.86 per pound, close enough that $127.47 ÷ 33 and $135.15 ÷ 35 land within a fraction of a cent of each other. EUHOMY, the most expensive of the three on the sticker, is actually the cheapest per claimed pound: $137.70 for 40 lbs comes out to roughly $3.44 a pound, about forty cents less per pound than either of the other two.

Read as a straight price-per-pound comparison, EUHOMY wins outright. But a daily-output figure, as we've covered in more depth in why daily output and storage are different numbers, is the ceiling a manufacturer prints assuming the reservoir stays topped up for a full 24 hours straight, which almost nobody's kitchen actually does. Treat 33, 35 and 40 as rough ceilings rather than numbers you'll see reproduced on your own counter, and the $10.23 spread between these three machines stops being explained by output at all. Something else in each listing is absorbing that money, and that's what the rest of this comparison is about.

What the middle price buys

Typhur's pump and eight cleaner packs, against a plain self-clean button

The Typhur Nugget Ice Maker Countertop is the one machine here built around a specific mechanical claim: a high-pressure self-cleaning pump, backed by eight included cleaner packs in the box. That's a different proposition from the plain self-clean button most budget nugget ice makers ship with, which typically just recirculates the same water through the system for a longer cycle. A pump-driven system paired with dedicated cleaner packets is built to flush an actual descaling solution through the lines under pressure — closer to running a real descaling cycle than simply rinsing with hot water on repeat.

Eight packs in the box changes the ongoing-cost math too. A machine that ships with one cycle's worth of cleaner is betting you'll remember to reorder; shipping with eight sets up roughly eight months of monthly cleanings before that becomes a decision you have to make. These machines genuinely need regular descaling — they recirculate the same water continuously, and mineral buildup is the single most common reason a nugget ice maker that worked fine on day one is slower by month three. Whether the pump and the included packs justify Typhur sitting $7.68 above Silonn and $2.55 below EUHOMY depends on how much you'd otherwise spend on a separate descaling product, and how often you'd actually remember to run it.

The speed claim

EUHOMY's four-minute first batch, against two listings that don't state a time

EUHOMY's own listing title is explicit about speed in a way the other two aren't: it states ice in about four minutes for the first batch. That's fast for this category. Countertop nugget ice makers in the $120-to-$150 range generally quote somewhere in a six-to-ten-minute range for a first batch, as a rough category benchmark rather than anything specific to Silonn or Typhur — neither of those two listings states a first-batch time for the models compared here, so we won't assign either one a number we don't have. What we can say honestly is that a stated four minutes is meaningfully faster than the unstated-but-typical range machines at this price usually fall into.

Four minutes versus an unstated time in that six-to-ten-minute band matters most at one specific moment: the first glass of the day, or the first round when people show up unannounced. It matters far less once a machine has been running for an hour, because by then all three are cycling continuously and the gap between a four-minute start and a slower one has already washed out. If the first pour is the moment you actually care about — a Saturday brunch, a surprise guest — EUHOMY's stated number is the one real speed advantage on this page. If your household turns the machine on once in the morning and lets it run, the difference stops mattering by the second batch.

The spec in two different units

1.5 liters, 1.1 liters and 1.3 quarts — compared on the same scale

This is the comparison where the listings make the job harder than it needs to be. Typhur states a 1.5-liter tank. Silonn states 1.1 liters. EUHOMY states its reservoir in a different unit entirely — 1.3 quarts — which nobody can put next to the other two figures without converting it first. A US liquid quart is about 0.946 liters, so EUHOMY's 1.3 quarts works out to roughly 1.23 liters: bigger than Silonn's 1.1, smaller than Typhur's 1.5, and not nearly as easy to place on the shelf as the two round liter figures sitting on either side of it.

Once all three sit on the same scale, the ranking is Typhur largest at 1.5 L, EUHOMY in the middle at roughly 1.23 L, and Silonn smallest at 1.1 L — a spread of about 400 milliliters, less than a glass of water, between the biggest tank and the smallest. None of these is a large-capacity machine, and none is marketed as one. What the gap actually buys is runtime between refills, not a meaningfully bigger standing reserve. A smaller tank on a machine quoting a higher daily-output number, like EUHOMY's, simply means the reservoir gets topped up more often to reach that stated ceiling; a smaller tank on a lower-output machine, like Silonn's, means fewer total refills are needed to hit its own lower number. Treat the figure as a refill-frequency spec rather than a storage spec, and the mismatched units stop mattering as much as the fact that none of these three is built for long unattended stretches, regardless of which unit its tank happens to be printed in.

The name on the box

A brand nobody's heard of, priced against two that have a track record

Typhur is the one name in this comparison that doesn't show up anywhere else on this site. Silonn and EUHOMY both do: we've covered Silonn's self-dispensing nugget ice maker as a separate flagship model in that brand's lineup, and compared EUHOMY's nugget ice makers against its own bullet-ice machines in a different guide, because both brands have built out enough of a catalog in this category to leave a track record beyond one listing. Typhur, by contrast, shows up here as a single product with one specific mechanical pitch — the cleaning pump and the eight packs — and nothing else in our research to set next to it.

That's not automatically a mark against it. Every established budget brand in this category was an unknown name the first time it listed a nugget ice maker, and a company's first product can be its best-engineered one precisely because there's no older model's reputation riding on the name yet. It is, though, a real difference in what you're actually buying into. Choosing Silonn or EUHOMY at this price means choosing a brand with other models and other listings you can cross-check if something about this specific one looks off — the same kind of track-record question we raise in whether an Amazon Renewed nugget ice maker is worth the discount, another case where the brand's history is doing real work in the decision, not just the spec sheet. Choosing Typhur means betting on the pump-and-packs pitch standing on its own, with no sibling listing to compare it against if this first one disappoints.

Our method

How we compared — and what we can’t tell you

What we did

Three listings, the same four questions

We compared the published titles, stated daily output, stated tank volume and current Amazon prices for the Silonn, Typhur and EUHOMY countertop nugget ice makers above, then converted EUHOMY's 1.3-quart tank into liters so all three sit on one scale. Where a listing states a feature directly — a cleaning pump, a first-batch time, a tank size — we report it as stated. Where a spec such as a first-batch time isn't in the data we have for a given model, we say so rather than estimate one.

What we didn’t

We don’t run a testing lab

We do not physically test these machines, we do not own them, and we have no owner reviews or star ratings on file for any of the three ASINs compared here, so none are cited or implied. What we can do honestly is take each listing's own claims at face value, set them against the price difference, and be explicit about which numbers are published specifications and which are simply absent from the data we have.

FAQ

Common questions

Why are Typhur, Silonn and EUHOMY priced so close together?

They aren't tracking a shared daily-output number — that ranges from 33 to 40 lbs across the three. The $10.23 total spread tracks what each brand added instead: Typhur's cleaning pump and eight included cleaner packs, EUHOMY's higher stated output and faster first batch, and Silonn's position as the plainest, cheapest of the three. Priced per claimed pound, Silonn and Typhur land within a fraction of a cent of each other at about $3.86, while EUHOMY is the cheapest per pound at roughly $3.44 despite having the highest sticker price.

Which of the three has the biggest water tank?

Once all three are converted to the same unit, Typhur's stated 1.5 liters is the largest, EUHOMY's 1.3 quarts converts to roughly 1.23 liters for second place, and Silonn's stated 1.1 liters is the smallest. The gap between the biggest and smallest is about 400 milliliters — less than a glass of water — so treat it as a difference in how often you'll refill, not in how much ice any of them can hold in reserve.

How much faster is EUHOMY's first batch, really?

EUHOMY's own listing states ice in about four minutes for the first batch, which is faster than the roughly six-to-ten-minute range typical of countertop nugget ice makers in this price bracket generally. Neither the Silonn nor the Typhur listing compared here states a first-batch time, so we can't put an exact number on either of those two; what's clear is that EUHOMY is the only one of the three making a specific, fast claim on this point.

Is Typhur, as a new brand, a riskier buy than Silonn or EUHOMY?

It's a different kind of risk, not necessarily a bigger one. Silonn and EUHOMY both have other models covered elsewhere on this site, which gives you a track record to check if something about either listing looks off. Typhur shows up here as a single product with one specific mechanical pitch — the high-pressure cleaning pump and the eight included cleaner packs — and no sibling listing from the same brand to compare it against. That's a real trade-off to weigh, not a reason to rule it out on its own.
