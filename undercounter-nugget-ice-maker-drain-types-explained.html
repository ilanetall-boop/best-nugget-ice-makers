Buying Guide · Undercounter Nugget Ice Makers

Gravity, electric or pump: the drain your cabinet picks for you.

A countertop nugget ice maker never asks this question. A built-in one asks it before you can even order the thing: how does the melt and waste water actually leave the unit? Three undercounter machines in our database each state a different answer in their own product name — gravity drainage, electric drainage and a drain pump — at three different prices that don't track with daily output. We compared what each listing actually states, and what that commits you to behind the cabinet wall.

Every countertop nugget ice maker on the market solves drainage the same lazy, convenient way: it doesn't drain anywhere. The melt water stays inside a sealed reservoir, gets reused to make the next batch, and the only plumbing decision you make is deciding where on the counter the thing sits. That's true of the GE Profile Opal, the Silonn models, the EUHOMY countertop units — all of it. You plug it in, you pour water in the top, and you never think about where the waste goes, because there isn't any that leaves the box.

An undercounter, built-in nugget ice maker breaks that arrangement on purpose. It's designed to sit permanently inside a cabinet cutout, the same way a dishwasher or a wine fridge does, and like those appliances it needs somewhere for wastewater to actually go — out of the unit, through a line, and into your home's plumbing. That's not a footnote in the spec sheet. It's the first installation question you have to answer, and it's one a countertop machine simply never forces on you. For a wider look at what else changes once a nugget ice maker moves from the counter into a cabinet, see our guide to built-in nugget ice makers for an outdoor kitchen or wet bar.

Three undercounter machines in our database make the drain question concrete, because each one names its drainage method directly in the product title rather than burying it in a specifications table. An EUHOMY built-in model states gravity drainage. A VEVOR built-in model states electric drainage. A second VEVOR built-in model states it comes with a drain pump. Three different named methods, from two brands, at three different prices — and as we'll get to, the price doesn't line up with the daily output the way you'd expect. What it lines up with is the drain.

Method #1

Gravity drainage: the EUHOMY, and what's behind the cabinet wall

The EUHOMY Under Counter Nugget Ice Maker Machine, 66Lbs/Day, listed with gravity drainage, is priced at $839.99. Gravity drainage is the simplest drain method that exists for any plumbed appliance: there's no pump, no motor, no electrically driven mechanism moving the wastewater anywhere. The water simply runs downhill, the whole distance, from the unit to wherever it's meant to end up — a floor drain, a standpipe, or a connection into existing drain plumbing.

The trade a gravity system makes is that "downhill the whole distance" has to be literally true, every inch of the way, or it backs up. A gravity drain line needs a continuous downward slope from the unit to the drain point, with no low spots or uphill sections in between, and it needs enough physical height behind or under the cabinet to establish that slope before the line ever reaches the floor. That's a real constraint on where a gravity-drain undercounter unit can go: if your cabinet sits over a finished floor with no floor drain nearby, or if the drain point available to you sits level with or above the unit's own outlet, gravity alone has nothing to work with. EUHOMY's own listing is the authority on this specific unit's physical dimensions and outlet placement — we're describing how gravity drainage works as a category, not asserting a slope figure this particular listing doesn't publish, so check the installation diagram on the product page against your own cabinet before you order.

What gravity buys you in exchange is one less moving part. There's no pump to wear out, no pump motor drawing power, and nothing mechanical sitting between the unit and the drain line that can fail on its own. If your cabinet already has the vertical clearance and a drain point lower than the unit, gravity is the plainest, least complicated way to get water out of a built-in ice maker.

Method #2

Electric drainage: the $714.99 VEVOR, and where the unit can sit

The VEVOR Undercounter Built-in Ice Maker, 80 Lbs/Day, states electric drainage in its own product name, and it's priced at $714.99 — the lowest price of the three machines in this guide, despite the highest stated daily output. VEVOR's listing names the method as "Electric Drainage" without walking through the internal mechanism in the detail we'd need to describe it precisely, and we're not going to fill that gap with a guess about exactly how the electrical component moves the water. What the name does tell you, reliably, is that this isn't a pure gravity system: there's an electrically driven element involved in getting wastewater out, rather than relying solely on a downhill slope.

That distinction matters for where the unit is allowed to live. A machine that depends entirely on gravity needs its outlet positioned higher than the drain point, full stop. A machine with an electrically assisted drain is less rigidly tied to that geometry, because the electrical component is doing some of the work gravity would otherwise have to do alone. In practice, that generally gives an electric-drainage unit more flexibility in cabinet placement than a pure gravity system, particularly in a kitchen or bar layout where the available drain connection isn't conveniently lower than the ice maker itself.

The honest caveat is the same one we'd apply to any spec we can't verify directly: confirm the exact installation requirements, including any maximum run length or vertical lift the electric drainage can handle, against VEVOR's own installation documentation for this listing before you commit to a cabinet layout. The name tells you the category of solution; it doesn't substitute for the installation manual.

Method #3

Drain pump: the $999.90 VEVOR, and why pumping costs more

The VEVOR Under Counter Ice Maker, 66 Lbs/Day, listed with a drain pump, is the most expensive of the three machines in this guide at $999.90 — roughly $285 more than the electric-drainage VEVOR and about $160 more than the gravity-drainage EUHOMY, despite stating the identical 66 lbs/day output as the EUHOMY. A drain pump is the most capable solution of the three on paper, because a dedicated pump can actively push wastewater uphill or over a longer horizontal run than gravity could ever manage on its own, which generally means the unit can be installed in locations neither a gravity system nor an electric-assist system could reach — furthest from any existing drain, in an island, or wherever the available drain connection simply isn't downhill of the cabinet.

That capability is also exactly why a drain pump system costs more to build. A dedicated pump is an added mechanical component — its own motor, its own moving parts, its own point of failure over the appliance's lifetime — that neither a gravity system nor an electric-drainage design needs. Manufacturers pass that added hardware cost on, and on this specific trio of listings, the drain pump model is priced highest of the three by a clear margin even though its stated daily output doesn't exceed the cheaper gravity unit's. You're not paying more here for more ice. You're paying for installation flexibility: the ability to put the unit where a gravity line or an electric-assist line couldn't reach.

If your cabinet has no nearby drain that's lower than the unit, and no realistic way to create one, a drain pump is the method built to solve exactly that problem. If your cabinet already has a convenient lower drain point, you'd be paying the premium for flexibility you don't actually need.

The number that misleads

66 and 80 lbs a day don't track with which drain type was chosen

It would be reasonable to assume that a more expensive drainage method comes bundled with a more capable machine, the way a pricier car trim usually comes with a bigger engine. These three listings make the opposite case. The $714.99 VEVOR with electric drainage states the highest daily output of the three at 80 lbs/day, and it's the cheapest. The $839.99 EUHOMY with gravity drainage states 66 lbs/day — the same stated output as the $999.90 VEVOR with a drain pump, which costs roughly $160 more than the EUHOMY and $285 more than the 80 lb/day VEVOR, for an identical headline number.

Lay the three side by side and the price climbs almost entirely with drain complexity, not with how much ice the machine makes in a day: gravity and electric drainage sit at the low and lowest price points respectively, and the drain pump model sits at the top of the range while matching, not beating, the gravity unit's output. If you shopped this category by daily-output number alone, sorting lowest to highest price, you'd expect the $999.90 machine to out-produce the $714.99 one. It doesn't. The lbs/day figure is a real, published spec, but on this trio it isn't the variable the price is actually responding to. We've made a version of this point before about a different number entirely — see our breakdown of why daily output and bin size are different numbers that don't move together either. The lesson generalizes: read each spec for what it actually measures, not for what you'd assume it implies about the others.

What the price is tracking, on this specific trio, is the drain hardware: no pump at all (gravity) and an electrically assisted drain sit cheaper, a dedicated mechanical pump sits dearer. That's a pattern worth checking against any other undercounter model you're comparing, rather than assuming daily output alone explains a price gap. FAQ

Common questions

Why does a built-in nugget ice maker need a drain at all?

Because, unlike a countertop machine, it's designed to sit permanently inside a cabinet cutout rather than on an open counter where you can pull it out to empty a reservoir. Countertop nugget makers recirculate their melt water internally and never drain anywhere; built-in undercounter units are plumbed appliances, the same category as a dishwasher or an undercounter wine fridge, and they need an actual route for wastewater to leave the unit. That's the one installation question a countertop machine never forces you to answer.

What does gravity drainage actually require behind the cabinet?

A continuous downward slope for the entire length of the drain line, with no low points or uphill sections, and enough physical height behind or under the cabinet to establish that slope before the line reaches a floor drain or standpipe. If the available drain connection in your kitchen sits level with or above the unit's outlet, gravity alone has nothing to work with, and you'd need an electric-assist or pump system instead.

Is electric drainage the same as a drain pump?

Not according to how these two listings name themselves. The $714.99 VEVOR states "Electric Drainage" while the $999.90 VEVOR states it comes "with Drain Pump" — two distinct terms from the same brand, which suggests two distinct mechanisms, though neither listing details the internal difference precisely enough for us to describe it beyond the name. What's clear from the prices and from how each term is generally used in plumbed appliances is that a dedicated pump is the more capable, more expensive hardware of the two, built to move wastewater further or against gravity; confirm the specifics of either against the manufacturer's installation documentation before you buy.

Does a higher lbs-per-day rating mean a different drain type?

No, and this trio is the clearest evidence of that. The cheapest machine here, the $714.99 VEVOR with electric drainage, states the highest output at 80 lbs/day. The two 66 lbs/day machines — the EUHOMY and the drain-pump VEVOR — are priced $160 apart from each other despite an identical stated output. Daily output and drain type are two separate specifications that happen to sit on the same three listings; neither predicts the other.
